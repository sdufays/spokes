# Learning with Legos

[Learning with Legos](http://spokesamerica.tumblr.com/post/92925151075/learning-with-legos "Learning with Legos")

Hello from St. Louis - Emma here. I wanted to follow up Jamin’s recent blog post with a report on our latest teaching adventures, first in Loveland, CO at a library and then at the Amelia Earhart festival in Atchison, KS. 

In both teaching stops, we used an open format where each of us had our own table space, and kids could walk up to any of us to learn about what interested them the most. My workshop is focused on introducing students to the basic concepts driving lean manufacturing and designing for manufacturing. I use Lego blocks to represent the parts or sub-assemblies that my budding engineers must assemble into robots, spaceships or other creations that can be mass-produced. 

In our four stops so far, I have continued to learn and develop my workshop. Here are some of the things my students have taught me: 

My designs are stupid. The robot I designed and assigned to the kids to mass produce falls apart and is tricky to assemble. In my first workshops, my students were constantly complaining about it. Finally, I challenged them to build a better design, and voila - an integral part of my workshop was born. Now, instead of dictating the design, I gave the kids the freedom to use their creativity to design something new. I also gained an opportunity to teach another important aspect of manufacturing, which is design for manufacturing and assembly (DFA for short). 

![](http://33.media.tumblr.com/681b7c10a91bc948129909eaff002206/tumblr_inline_n9bs8bwD7w1s3e7lq.jpg)

Lego allows you to “meet the students where they are.” One of the parents in Atchison used this phrase, and I couldn’t agree more. In Atchison, I dumped all my Lego blocks on a table and as kids walked by, I asked them to design their own robot for manufacturing. Once they had something built, we could talk about how to make their particular design better - can we make the leg sub-assembly more robust? Could we replace those two little blocks with a bigger one so that it would be faster to assemble? Can we make the design symmetrical so that the factory worker couldn’t make a mistake in assembling the sub-components? I found this strategy effective because I was able to communicate fundamental DFA concepts that I learned in college to 8 year olds - they saw my explanations as common sense because it was obvious how the changes we implemented made their Lego designs easier to manufacture. 

![](http://33.media.tumblr.com/d4968240090490869562a6144bae31b4/tumblr_inline_n9bs9060Sk1s3e7lq.jpg)

Lego is a universal language that every 8 year old speaks. Especially in Atchison where my Lego blocks were tantalizingly laid out on a table, I have learned that EVERYONE knows how to play with Lego. I wish I could claim that I am a genius and I knew all along that Legos would be a magical tool, but really I just chose to use them as a teaching vehicle because that was how I learned about DFA. Regardless, the Legos have been the critical hook that draws kids to my workshop, even at the Amelia Earhart festival where there were lots of competing distractions. This is incredibly valuable beyond just attracting crowds of kids to my table; it also means that none of my students are afraid to engage with my workshop because they already know how Legos work, and they’re not intimidated. That’s a stark contrast from some of my engineering classes in college, where the hardest part of the class was convincing myself that I could operate a scary and powerful lathe. But at my workshop table, the Legos are a universal attraction for everyone, including girls, younger students, and kids whose first language isn’t English - Legos may be the best toy in the world for getting young kids interested in STEM. 

Legos are a gateway drug to more in-depth learning about mechanical engineering. When I ran my workshop at the Amelia Earhart festival, I had stolen a bicycle wheel hub from Maddie’s table. We’d taken apart the hub that morning, and you could see how the ball bearings were packed inside, and how the manufacturer had designed a special nut that both fixed the hub on the axle and served as the inner race for the bearings. I know what you’re thinking: “wow Emma, DFA is everywhere!” - it totally is! :) After spending a couple minutes talking about DFA concepts with the kids’ Lego designs, I would transition to showing them this wheel hub, taking it apart to show them the insides. I thought that was valuable for them to have a real world example, but I think the critical part of using the hub was that once I had taken it apart, I asked the kids to put it back together. Soon I had 8 year olds packing bearings, assembling wheel hubs and getting grease all over their fingers; they didn’t realize it, but they’d fearlessly taken apart and put back together a mechanical device. I didn’t learn to do that until my senior year of college, but here were these kids doing it like it was the most natural thing in the world. If there is one thing I hope they take away from my workshop, its that they don’t have to be afraid of taking things apart. I know that I learned the most about engineering when I was told to take apart lawn mower engines and transmissions, and I believe that fearlessly tinkering and taking things apart can help anybody learn more about mechanical engineering. In fact, taking things apart should seem as much fun and as easy to do as playing with Legos.
