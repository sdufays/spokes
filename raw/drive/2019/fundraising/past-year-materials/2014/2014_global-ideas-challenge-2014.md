## Scope statement (Feb 25 v2)

MIT IDEAS Global Challenge: 
Scope Statement

	Spokes America: 2014 The Spokes 2014 team mission is to inspire students across the United States to develop a greater interest in science and engineering by bicycling across the country and teaching short technical workshops along the way. 

Background
In the United States today, the K-12 education system is struggling to adequately prepare students to enter technical fields such as the physical sciences, computer programming, and engineering. In the next decade, an increasing number of technical positions in the US will go unfilled unless we improve access to education in STEM subjects and inspire students to pursue studies in those fields. 
There are already many resources available for teaching K-12 students to master technical skills – there are online games that students can play to learn mathematics; there are virtual academies where students can watch lectures and develop familiarity with coding languages; there is an entire industry producing hobbyist electrical components that beginners can assemble in their own projects. But teachers and students do not always know how to take advantage of these resources, or how to use them to their full potential. Spokes’ plan is to take these resources into the classroom and to collaborate with teachers to make them an integral part of their teaching plans. 
Our target community will be middle schools across the country in both rural and urban areas. We will be working with both teachers and their students to develop innovative ways of teaching STEM subjects by connecting schools to various online resources and bringing “Maker Kits” with hobby electronics materials to allow students to continue to learn after we leave. We will organize one- or two-day “learning festivals” which will excite students about STEM fields and demonstrate how students can use online resources and hobbyist electronics to learn about science and engineering in a hands-on and exciting way. We will be building upon the work of the Spokes 2013 team, which organized the first iteration of this project last summer. 

Project Description
Our goal is to teach at approximately ten different schools over the course of the summer, and based on last year’s experiences, we will reach between 30 and 80 students at each school – last year, Spokes 2013 taught approximately 425 students in total. Team members will also be documenting their experiences on social media and on film so that we can inspire a wider audience to explore STEM subjects with the same resources that we will demonstrate in classrooms. In addition, we plan on expanding our organization’s website to include a repository of online educational resources, as well as copies of ourour lesson plans. 
We plan to build a network of teachers at the schools we visit so that they can continue to develop new ways of teaching STEM after the summer ends, most likely on an Internet forum or a group mailing list. We are focusing on reaching under-resourced areas who will have the most to gain from access to free online resources to teach STEM subjects, thus many of our teaching stops will be in the rural Midwest. 
Our project will consist of a cross-country bicycle journey, with frequent stops at local schools, libraries, and other community centers where we will conduct “learning festivals” that are focused on teaching subjects in science and engineering. 
We chose to travel by bicycle because we have learned from the Spokes 2013 team that it primes the students to be excited to learn from us. There is a certain novelty in learning from people who biked to the school, and we want to capitalize on that novelty to build up the students’ excitement about our curriculum. The bicycles will also be used as a primary teaching tool for understanding basic physics and engineering design. Specifically, we will be tinkering with different gears and exploring the basis of the bicycle design.  We will also use the bicycle as a basis for small design challenges such as building smoothie blenders.Finally, we hope that by bicycling in to various schools, we will challenge the prevalent stereotype that people who like math and science cannot also be athletic and active. We want to prove to younger students that STEM is cool and that by studying STEM subjects you are exploring the world around you. 
Our journey will begin in San Francisco and will follow routes set by the Adventure Cycling Association across the country to Washington DC. Our “learning festivals” will be organized in collaboration with community partners, and Spokes team members will teach interconnected classes to middle school students (please see Appendix 1 for a summary of our proposed curriculum). We have many sponsors and partners who will support our classes, including Raspberry Pi and Khan Academy. We will publicize our work through blog posts, video posts, and, potentially, by presenting our findings to Department of Education staff in Washington DC at the culmination of our trip. 
In our research we have found that there are some traveling shows in existence that focus on inspiring students to study STEM subjects. For example, there is the US Science and Engineering Traveling Festival and the University of Illinois Physics Van. While these are excellent outreach programs, they are based on performance and interactive games, much like museum exhibits. Spokes, on the other hand, has a different teaching mission. We empower our students to teach themselves mathematics, computer programming or engineering. We want to show them that building, tinkering, creating and exploring is a way of life, and that there are resources available for them to create anything they want, even if they do not have access to laboratories or machine shops. We want to change the way STEM is taught in the classroom, such that students are not just inspired for a weekend, but for a lifetime of learning.

We foresee many challenges in different aspects of our project. With respect to logistics, we will need to find housing along our route. To address this, we will be carrying camping equipment in our van, and we will also be staying with friends and contacts along the way.

A van will be used to carrying extra bicycle parts and tools, first aid kits and medicines; we can also transport injured or sick team members in the van. Our team members will all be trained in basic bicycle repairs, basic automobile repair (like changing a tire) and first aid/CPR. Appendix 1: Curriculum Outline

Format
Spokes will be teaching to students in a formal classroom setting, as well as to summer programs in libraries, community centers and camps. To best serve these different environments, we will offer half-day to full-day workshops to schools, and one- to two-day weekend workshops to summer programs outside of schools. The descriptions below list the classes that Spokes team members will be co-teaching with each other; we will combine the classes as appropriate for each environment we are in.
 Build and Drive a Raspberry Pi Car Workshop
	In morning-afternoon or other two session style workshops, students will be exposed to basic ideas from mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, and computer science. In the first session, students will work with Raspberry Pi hardware and appropriate components to build a basic vehicle. This first workshop will introduce design thinking ideas, teaching students to think about why their devices are built the way they are and will familiarize them with working with components of mechanical and electrical devices. 
	In the afternoon, as a motivating exercise for basic exploration of the power of computer science, students will use the Pi programming language to interact with the hardware they constructed in the first session. The purpose of this session will be two-fold. First, students will be exposed to how humans interact with electronics and computer at a lower level than they usually do. For example, they will peel back the hood of a mechanical and electrical device and see how engineers designed the electronics to perform a specific function. The idea is that students learn that computers were not just “made”; real people designed and created the infrastructure and the software that lets them surf the web and play games, and creating these things is something they can learn how to do! Second, students will be exposed to the power of computers and a properly programmed device to interact with the physical world. To do this, students will program their vehicles to drive along a set course that they map out on the ground with tape. At the end we will host a competition to see which student or students made their car drive the course fastest or most efficiently! 

Math and Games Workshop
During the mathematics workshops, students will be exposed to different concepts of game theory and asked to analyze the games that they played. Different topics will be introduced as games and students will have the opportunity to play against each other or even challenge the lecturer if time allows. Through hands-on exploration students will be able to devise winning strategies for games such as nim, chomp, and tic-tac-toe.
Furthermore, students will also be exposed to games with inherent positive or negative values associated with multiple different outcomes. Such games include the classic prisoner’s dilemma. Following exposure to these more complicated logic games, the class will conduct a simulation with candy as prizes. Additionally, a simulation will be demonstrated on Netlogo to depict the different possible outcomes on a larger sale.
This workshop will serve to encourage students to think critically and logically about mathematical concepts without experiencing the negative connotations that the word “math” often carries for them in school.

Circuits Workshop
Students will design and make electronic games using basic circuit elements, logic components, and a microcontroller. Students will be exposed to basic electrical engineering topics such as truth tables, Boolean algebra and circuit analysis. Students will also learn simple programming, circuit debugging and how to use a breadboard. 
Possible games to design include rock paper scissors, whack-a-mole or another open-ended design. By leaving circuit kits at the schools, students can use what they learn to create more new games after the workshops end.

Climbing Mechanics
	Through rock climbing students will gain intuition about mechanics principles such as force, torque and friction. By changing their body position and points of contact on a wall, students will directly experience forces and torques, and then do a free body analysis. Students can also learn about friction coefficients by experimenting with chalk. Biomechanics and hand anatomy will also be studied, exposing students to other mechanics topics including stress, and spring laws. 
 Estimation Physics 
	How much energy do you output when you bicycle for an hour? How much power does a human output through heat? When watching a crazy stunt in a movie, do you wonder is that really possible? How could you check it?
       	Students will work with basic force balance and dimensional analysis to learn how to estimate things relevant to their daily lives. Emphasis will be placed on the process – asking the relevant questions, breaking the problem down into parts, making comparisons and order of magnitude estimates. These are good activities to brainstorm as a class, to have students compare separate answers, or to allow each student to explore their own interests.

Informal Activities (Outside the Workshops)
In addition to these official activities for students, we look to engage the broader communities we stay with through informal activities outside of the classroom. To help develop a community of tinkerers, we plan to use public spaces to demo creative and sustainable engineering projects, such as a bicycle-powered smoothie blender and other contraptions.  To encourage communities to be curious about the world around them, and the beauty contained therein, we will use a portable telescope for daytime observations of the sun and nighttime observations of the stars - topics which capture the minds of young and old alike. These projects would be easy to run, would reach more than just students, while generating interest in tinkering and exploring, showing that anyone can engage in their own explorations.
