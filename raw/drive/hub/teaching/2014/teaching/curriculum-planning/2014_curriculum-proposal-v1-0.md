	In addition to these official activities for students, we look to engage the broader communities we stay with through informal activities outside of the classroom. To help develop a community of tinkerers, we plan to use public spaces to demo creative and sustainable engineering projects, such as a bike-powered smoothie blender, and other contraptions.  To encourage communities to be curious of the world around them, and the beauty contained therein, we will be able to use a portable telescope for daytime observations of the sun and nighttime observations of the stars -- topics which capture the minds of young and old alike. These projects would be easy to run, would reach more than just students, while generating interest in tinkering and exploring, showing that anyone can engage in similar projects.

MechE/EE/CS Workshop (Sam)
	In a morning-afternoon or other two session style workshops, students will be exposed to basic ideas from MechE, EE, and CS. In the first or morning sessions, students will work with Raspberry Pi hardware and appropriate components to build a basic vehicle. This first workshop will introduce design thinking ideas, teaching students to think about why their devices are built the way they are and will familiarize them with working with components of mechanical and electrical devices (Note to Spokes: I’m not an engineer so if someone has a better way to express this, go for it!).
	In the afternoon, as a motivating exercise for basic exploration of the power of computer science, students will use the Pi programming language to interact with the hardware they constructed in the first session. The purpose of this session will be two-fold. First, students will be exposed to how humans interact with electronics and computer at a lower-level than they usually do—i.e. they’ll peel back the hood of a mechanical and electrical device and see “how” people made their electronics do what they’re supposed to do. The idea is that students learn that computers aren’t just “there”; someone out there in the world designed and created the infrastructure and the software that lets them surf the web and play games, and creating these things is something they can learn how to do! Second, students will be exposed to the power of computers and a properly programmed device to interact with the physical world. To do this, students will program their vehicles to drive along a set course that they map out on the ground with tape. Perhaps at the end we’ll see which student or students made their car drive the course fastest or most efficiently!
	I don’t know a lot about Raspberry Pi or the MechE/EE logistics of how this workshop would go, but the second half of this is really how I would love to go about introducing CS ideas for middle schoolers or really pretty much anyone. Positive feedback via a computer’s interaction with the physical world is really the best way to impress a student with the power of computer science...so if someone can figure out how to make the first half of this work, that would be awesome. I’m operating on the assumption the Raspberry Pi is functionally similar to Arduino (which a quick google revealed it to be). Make changes!!!

Math Workshop (aka Games Workshop)
During the mathematics workshops, students will be exposed to different concepts of game theory and asked to analyze the games that they played. Different topics will be introduced as games and students would have the opportunity to play against each other or even challenge the lecturer if time allows. Through hands-on exploration students will be able to devise winning strategies for games such as nim, chomp, and tic-tac-toe.
Furthermore, students will also be exposed to games with inherent positive or negative values associated with multiple different outcomes. Such games include the classic prisoner’s dilemma. Following exposure to these more complicated logic games, the class will conduct a simulation with candy as prizes. Additionally, a simulation will be demonstrated on Netlogo to depict the different possible outcomes on a larger sale.
This workshop will serve to encourage students to think critically and logically on mathematical concepts without being affected by the negative feelings that the word “math” often carries for them in schools.

Circuits Workshop
Students will design and make electronic games using basic circuit elements, logic components, and a microcontroller. Students will be exposed to basic electrical engineering topics such as truth tables, boolean algebra and circuit analysis. Students will also learn simple programming, circuit debugging and how to use a breadboard.
Possible games include rock paper scissors, whack-a-mole or anything the student can think of. By leaving circuit kits at the schools, students can use what they learn to create new games after the workshops.

Climbing Mechanics (Still deciding...If you just want one per person, choose whichever you think sounds better)
	Through rock climbing students will learn and gain intuition about mechanicals principal such as force, torque and friction. By changing their body position and points of contact on a  wall, students will directly experience forces and torques effects then do a free body analysis. Students can also learn about friction coefficients by experimenting with chalk usage.
	Biomechanics and hand anatomy can also be studied, exposing students to other mechanics topics including stress, and spring laws.

Estimation Physics (Cali: I’m picturing it to go sort of like this)
	How much energy do you output when you bicycle for an hour? How much power does a human output through heat? When watching a crazy stunt in a movie, do you wonder is that really possible? How could you check it?
        	Students will work with basic force balance and dimensional analysis to learn how to estimate things relevant to their lives. Emphasis will be placed on the process – asking the relevant questions, breaking it down into parts, making comparison and order of magnitude estimates.These are good activities to brainstorm as a class, to have students compare separate answers, or to allow each student to explore their own interests.
