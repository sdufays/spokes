Highlight Safety Areas:

Good Practices for Maintaining Awareness while Biking:
Don’t wear earbuds while biking - you need to use your ears to listen for traffic on the road
Keep your head up and look around - notice cars passing you, other bikes, and action on the sidewalk

Dog Safety:
In case of a Dog chasing you,
Spray dog with water bottle
If dog continues to chase, get off bike and put bike between yourself and dog
Don’t make eye contact with dog
Can also buy dog taser (high frequency sound that scares dog)
Or pepper spray

Bear Safety:
Run downhill if it’s a brown bear (?)

Nutrition on Bike:
People should always have food/water on them
Energy bars (e.g. clif), electrolytes in drink (e.g. scratch)
Two water bottles
Don’t overexert yourself - Know your limits.

Keeping your bike safe (and well maintained):
Never leave your bike unattended
Always lock your bike with both wheels locked
Regular chain cleaning/bike maintenance sessions
Have at least one checkup at a bike store along the way to ensure that important parts (e.g. brakes, gears) are fully functional

How to stop on the side of the road safely:
Put 10 feet between your bike and the road (Most bike/car accidents happen while bike is at standstill)
Try to stop at turnabouts, where there’s more room between you and traffic
Watch out for cars turning right especially if you are in a blindspot

Biking alone vs Biking in a group:
Never bike alone
Riders should be comfortable with riding in a group of other bikers
Do not draft another biker unless you ask them first

Bike and Build “Common Sense” Bike Rules:
1. Always wear your helmet and safety triangle. These items could save your life. Always always wear them.
2. Share the road and obey all traffic laws. On your bike, you are considered a moving vehicle, and are subject to the rules of the road: stopping at stop signs, obeying traffic signals, staying under the speed limit, etc.
3. Be predictable. Ride straight and as close to the side of the road as you feel comfortable. You are entitled to 18-24 inches of road. Use signals when able and applicable.
4. Communicate. Communicate with vehicles and other riders by using voice and hand signals.
5. Be aware. Scan for hazards. Take the lane if you feel you and or the car would be at risk if they passed. Windy, single lane roads are a good example of this.
6. Be visible. Assume that the cars don’t see you. The previously noted points of predictability and communication will help with this. Riders will ride only during daylight and are required to have a red flashing tail light on at all times. If the weather is poor, a bright rain jacket will go a long way.
7. Behave like a car. When you are questioning how you should behave at an intersection etc, consider what you would do if you were in a vehicle. In most cases, you should behave as if you were in a car. A quick example: When approaching a red light, you should wait in line like a vehicle, not snake between cars or squeeze along the right side.
8. Lane positioning. Own your lane! Your lane positioning plays a large role in how drivers treat you. Position yourself so that drivers can see you and respond to you appropriately.
9. No distractions! No music. No booze. No cell phones.
10. Group riding is not solo riding. As previously noted, be predictable, be aware, and communicate. You may never ride three abreast and paceelining will be permitted on a case by case basis. Before the summer, you should ride a few times with a group to practice how to ride in a group

SPOKES ESSENTIAL SAFETY RULES:
Be familiar with all of these BEFORE the biking begins!
Never forget these rules as you go along your trip!

Wear your Helmet
This rule is so important it’s first and gets its own spot. Never forget to wear your helmet!
This is the single most important article of clothing to remember while riding your bike. It could save your life.
Never Bike Alone
Biking alone increases visibility of you and your bike to cars
Obey all Traffic Laws

Communicate
Communicate with both the 2-ton vehicles on the road AND with your fellow bikers. This includes using voice and hand signals to let others know what you and your bike plan to do.
