Most non-profits are required to have a 1) president, 2) treasurer and 3) secretary. Duties of each of these positions are defined by federal, state regulations and the non-profit’s by laws.
Many states allow combining 2 of the 3 roles in one individual (typically one person acts as both treasurer and secretary). [1]

Directors vs officers:
Directors have no inherent authority/power - only exercised as part of the board [3]
Officers have some authority/power to fulfill their duties
Officers can be directors in many cases (depends on the state laws and non profit bylaws)

All officers:
Fiduciary duty: cannot make deals to benefit yourself or have conflicts of interest between themselves and the nonprofit
The board of directors elect and can dismiss officers

President:
President is the principal executive officer, reports directly to the board of directors
Overall supervision and control of all functions of the org
Presides over board of director meetings
General signature authority
Influences how the board spends it time and who is chosen as a board member [2]
Must review and understand by laws and articles of incorporation
Spokesperson for the non profit to the public
Recruits new board members
Appoints committee chairs
In charge of CEO and board member performance evaluations and succession planning

Treasurer:
Depending on the size of the org they either manage financial staff, consultants, or take care of the basics themselves
Basics include: maintaining a budget, keeping a general ledger, making bank deposits, writing checks
Draft and present the annual budget to the board
Present a treasurer’s report at board meetings
Check for errors/fraud
Sign checks
Sign the annual tax return on behalf of the nonprofit
You may be personally liable for damage done by fraud perpetrated by others if the IRS deems that you and the board did not perform fiduciary responsibility to exercise reasonable care over the org’s finances (you can carry directors’ and officers’ insurance to protect against this) [4]

Secretary:
Key is to facilitate communication between board, management, members
Give notice of meetings, facilitate distribution of agendas and meeting minutes
Be knowledgeable about organization’s records
Good to know something about state corporate law, etc.
Should know about notice requirements (legal things - if you miss one, you open the org up to challenge) and scheduling meetings
Helps the board of directors meetings run smoothly
Keep meeting minutes - important in case challenged in court or by the IRS
Maintain corporate records
Keep up with annual filing deadlines with the state and IRS [5]
