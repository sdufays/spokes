Hello there! Emma again, writing from a bookshop in Carbondale, IL. We just finished what I like to think of as the Hat Trick of the education world: 3 teaching stops in 6 days! We did day-long workshops with a homeschool group in Milstadt, IL; a maker space in Farmington, MO and a science center in Carbondale, IL.

Based on these recent experiences, I’ve encountered another aspect of teaching engineering that I wanted to write about: the importance of teamwork. In Farmington, everyone on Spokes started to notice how groups of kids that worked together in a workshop flourished compared to their peers. For example, I had a group of three girls in my Lego manufacturing workshop who were really good at working together in designing their own robot and then optimizing their manufacturing process to make as many robots as possible in two minutes. They were constantly suggesting improvements to each others’ strategies and designs, and they all communicated clearly and effectively with each other. By the end of the afternoon, they had set a new record for building 32 iterations of their 6-piece robot in under 2 minutes.

Sam noticed the same three girls helped each other to master his Launchpad Lightshow workshop. When he taught one of the three girls a concept, she would immediately share it with the other two. Likewise, Maddie noticed that when she was teaching tinkering with bike parts, some of the older kids would start teaching the younger ones concepts that the older kids could grasp, but that the younger ones still struggled with.

I loved seeing examples of teamwork in action because I think it was often overlooked in my engineering education in school. I had to seek out special programs dedicated to engineering teamwork and leadership to really work on my collaboration skills; my classmates who didn’t find those opportunities could go through 4 years of engineering school and never have explicit instruction on how to work with others. 

But when you think about it, teamwork is a fundamental tool for engineers. There is no way that one person can design and build a car, or an airplane, or an iPhone. And yet when we think of the stereotypical engineer, they’re nerds and geeks – people without social skills, people who are introverts, people who would rather talk to a computer than a real person. This is a perception we need to change in the STEM world: we need to show the public that if you have strong communication skills and you like working in teams, then you would make a fantastic engineer.

Going back to the teaching world, I would argue that using teamwork in teaching STEM can help students learn more effectively. Today, I was working on a one-on-one basis with students, and I noticed that there were a lot of kids who were very hesitant to try designing their own robot. They were afraid to strike out on their own into unknown territory, to go out on a limb and try to design something that may or may not work. But when my kids who were working in teams were asked to design a robot, they jumped right into the challenge. The difference was in having a team to support you; the kids in teams knew that if they couldn’t come up with the right answer themselves, then one of their teammates probably could. But the students who were acting as individuals had none of that security. And I found the same was true in my college classes – I always liked to do my problem sets in groups, so I could get help if I went astray; I liked to work with teammates in the machine shop for safety and advice; and even when I worked on solo projects, my classmates and I would share advice and ideas over lunch.

Teamwork gives engineers the courage to try new things and to take risks on new technology and new designs that they wouldn’t take on their own. In a team, you can always draw on the expertise and knowledge of the people around you. Best of all, you will always find that the team can produce more amazing and innovative ideas than any one person could have come up with on their own.
