2019 Learning Festivals & Reflections
There are many different reasons to do outreach / many different ways to make an impact. I think our learning festivals covered a wide array of these reasons and also gave us some pretty good experience (particularly for people with less education experience). I think for our trip, middle-school / early high school is where I feel like we made the most impact, but I think we got a large amount of elementary school kids (who are unfortunately usually the ones that require lots of attention/energy). I don't know if there's any way to actually get older kids (we told everyone to try and get MS/HS kids), but if I were to do it again, I'd try for that. I think we overall weren't fans of the Farmington/St Louis LFs and I think lots of team members weren't fans of the Kansas City LF. I think one thing we agreed on was that we had lots of LFs that were very clustered at the beginning / end meaning the middle had lots of dry spells where there was lots of biking. I think there were also lots of places we stopped that fit our target demographic of not having access to this kind of opportunity otherwise (unlike Farmington or Lindon) where it woud've made sense to teach (basically any of the smaller towns in Kansas or Colorado or Utah). On the other hand, continuity is important to make a lasting impact in a community. I think Hazard, KY is town that really shows how a strong connection can really mean a lot to a community.

Maile/Erin were in charge of communicating to LFs at the beginning. They did a great job! At some point, we transitioned to me (Evan) and Edgardo. While a couple of the LF coordinators were good with email, I probably should've just called others once I realized they were bad at emails. It was super important to have a spreadsheet to track statuses/keep information straight since each learning festival was a bit different (times, student ages, spaces we have, etc).

Class preparation was done really late. We struggled with funding well into April. Come May, people were too busy to prepare classes so we didn't get to actually do any practice / iteration. I wish we had started much earlier so I could've helped improve peoples workshops. I have lots of resources for how to design classes (I think some are in the google drive somewhere) since it's not too easy of a thing. Definitely would recommmend trying to finish before Spark 2019 (ESP). I'm also happy to help with class design/feedback.

Workshops
Its a good idea to teach in pairs and design your own curriculum, trying to cover a bunch of different topics and involve lots of hands-on activities. Keep in mind that activities need to be adaptable on the fly to cater to different age groups. You never know what you’re going to get! Also, often times our workshops need to be revised after teaching them once or twice. Don’t be afraid to rework and improve!

2022 Workshops
Electronics
Instructors: Franklin Zhang and Mariela Perez
Version 1 (Breadboarding): Students learn about how electricity works and gain an intuition behind voltage, current, and resistance using an analogy. They learn about different electrical components such as LEDs, wires, batteries, etc. Then we teach them how a breadboard works using a worksheet that they can write on to indicate where they see a closed loop of wires on a breadboard. After learning about breadboards, we give them materials to build a simple LED circuit with the option of adding buttons, buzzers, and potentiometers.
Version 2 (Paper Speaker): Students learn about how speakers and sound works. Students then build a speaker out of paper, wires, and magnets. They will cut pieces from a paper template for the speaker parts. Then wrap the coils to make an electromagnet. Assemble the different components and then plug the device into a phone or laptop and play some music. The music is quiet but you can hear it!
Physics of Music
Instructors: Robert Henning
Chromatography and Color
Instructors: Simone Lassar and Sophia Fang
Students learn about physical vs structural color and do some fun painting and chromatography activities to explore these two types of color. Real emphasis on hands on making and learning, and an opportunity to go outside to collect leaves for one of the chromatography activities.
3D Printing
Instructors: Maxwell Yun
In this workshop, students learn about real-world applications of 3d-printing, as well as the various 3d-printing technologies that make them possible. Students will have hands-on exposure to cutting-edge, modern 3d-printed parts, as well as watch a 3d-printer print a part in real time.
Solar Cell
Instructors: Sophia Fang and Simone Lassar
Students learn how a dye-sensitized solar cell works and make one of their own using raspberry or blackberry juice as the light-absorbing dye. After making the solar cells, students go outside and learn how to use a multimeter to measure the voltage when exposing the solar cells to sunlight.
Additional notes: probably more geared for high school students since most middle-schoolers haven’t taken chemistry yet
2019 Workshops
Design, Test, Repeat!
Instructors: Leah Yost and Caralyn Cutlip
This workshop focused on a mechanical engineering concept known as rapid prototyping, where engineers make quick prototypes out of basic materials (cardboard, foamboard, construction paper, straws, etc) in order to quickly test lots of ideas. There were two build challenges. In one, students built a device to keep a ping pong ball moving as long as possible. In the other, students worked together to make a quick prototype of a new carnival game that other groups tested. A discussion of why rapid prototyping is useful and the next stages of design followed the activity.
Decoding DNA
Instructors: Maile Jim and William Kuhl
This workshop explored the role and influence of DNA in who we are both as individuals and as a living creature. Students learned to extract DNA from a strawberry and perform gel electrophoresis, a common laboratory technique used in research worldwide that separates DNA based on size. Through analyzing the results of the gel to find identity of the mystery solutions, students learned that science is not always certain.
Intelligence: from the Brain to Self-Driving Cars!
Instructors: Evan Tey and Edgardo Letona
This workshop discussed the brain, intelligence, and artificial intelligence. Topics ranged from how sight, memory, and learning work, and how this information is used to make self-driving cars, smart computer assistants, and TV show recommendations. A demonstration of reading nervous signals and utilizing them followed with Evan controlling Edgardo’s arm through an electronic device. After, students created their own neural network to and tested it to see if the program could tell the difference between nice and mean things.
Fun with Chemistry
Instructors: Devin Seyler and Erin Leydon
This demonstration-based workshop showed how exciting and dynamic chemistry can be. Demonstrations involved connecting the theory behind chemical reactions with tangible results that showed students the impacts of chemistry in everyday life as well as exciting applications. Experiments included “The Whoosh Bottle” which has applications to rocket designs and “Elephant Toothpaste” among others. Younger students were also introduced to the scientific method and wrote down their hypotheses for each experiment.
