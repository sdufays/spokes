EXPERIMENT 20: DIY AUDIO SPEAKER
(Lab developed by physics major, Dock Rosenthal)

Background:
A solenoid is a cylindrical loop of wire with many turns or wraps.  If current is run through the wire, a magnetic field is created. If an alternating current is run through the wire, the magnetic field will alternate its direction inside the solenoid.  This is called an electromagnet.  An electromagnet placed near a permanent magnet will alternatively attract and repel as the direction of the current in the electromagnet changes.

A loop with current going in the clockwise direction will act like a magnet with north facing one way:

While a loop with counter clockwise-current acts like a magnet with north facing the other way:

When the current is alternated between clockwise and counterclockwise directions hundreds or thousands of times per second, the two magnets subsequently attract and repel each other at the same rate.  If the permanent magnet is fixed and the electromagnet is free to oscillate, these magnetic forces cause the electromagnet to move back and forth.

The Speaker:
A speaker is a combination of an electromagnet and permanent magnet.  When you attach a speaker cone (or some other sound-producing device) to the electromagnet, the oscillation of the electromagnet will cause sound waves to propagate through the air. The speaker cone directs the sound and makes it loud enough to be audible.

Materials List:

Building the Speaker:
Create a solenoid by wrapping wire 100 times around the rubber grommet. Leave 12 inches of unwrapped wire at the start and end of the wrapping so you can connect your speaker to a sound source.  Be careful when wrapping; the wire is brittle and will break fairly easily.
Place each end of wire on the table and gently scrape off the insulation from the last inch of wire with a razorblade.  Look for a slight change in color to know that you’ve succeeded.
Remove the central plastic nub on the bottom of the plastic container with your fingernail or razorblade.
Put the 2 magnets on either side of the bottom of the plastic container, using their magnetic attraction to hold them in place.
Using the double-sided sticky foam, afix the grommet to the magnet that is inside the plastic container.
Place the stem of the paper cup snuggly into the grommet.  The paper cup is your speaker cone.
Secure the paper cup to the plastic container by taping strips of paper that extend from the top of the cup to the sides of the plastic container.
Testing your speaker:
Using alligator clips, hook up your speaker to a signal generator.  Make sure you have the generator set to produce sine waves.  Change the amplitude and frequency produced by the generator to test the frequency response of your speaker.

Questions
What are the highest and lowest frequencies that cause your speaker to produce an audible sound?

What happens when you try a frequency that is too low to hear?  Does your speaker still respond?

Try using a different type of wave for your input, like a square wave.  How does the speaker respond?

A Woofer can reproduce sound with little distortion from 300 Hz down to 20 Hz. Is this speaker a woofer? How do you think you could change the speaker design to make it respond better to low frequencies? (Think about the wavelength of these sound waves).

Now hook the speaker up to the boom box in the back of the lab room.  How does your speaker perform?
