## Silicon Valley Bank letter (with curriculum outline)

Silicon Valley Bank
Hello from Spokes America! 
We are a group of MIT, Harvard, and Columbia University science and engineering students, with a mission to encourage school children across the US to be self-driven, creative and curious in their approach to science and engineering. Our science and engineering outreach program will consist of a cross country cycling trip from San Francisco to Washington D.C. that exemplifies those qualities of self-driven creativity and curiosity. We are asking you, Silicon Valley Bank, to join our cause by sponsoring our journey. 

The American education system is struggling to produce enough scientists, engineers, and software engineers, who can continue to drive technological development in the U.S. As a key player in the tech sector, SVB benefits from continued growth in STEM. Spokes will address this critical issue by entering classrooms and summer camps to connect students and teachers with new teaching resources like online programming tools and hobby electronics kits. We will use these tools in science and engineering workshops that encourage students to ask questions and use creative problem-solving to satisfy their own curiosity, with the end goal of inspiring them to become the next generation of scientists and engineers. We will be cycling in order to challenge the stereotype that students interested in STEM cannot also be athletic and active. The full outline of our curriculum plans are attached to this letter. 

Sponsors of the Spokes America 2014 trip are enabling young people across the country to better realize their own creative problem-solving potential, as well as connecting teachers to new teaching resources for science and technology, all for the sake of building a stronger economic foundation for the nation. We believe that Spokes’ goals align both with SVB’s role in the future of American technology and its institutional belief in the power of cycling. 
 
  I look forward to discussing Spokes in more detail with you. Please feel free to contact me or visit our website at www.spokesamerica.org/, and you can watch some footage about Spokes and last year’s successful trip at here and here.

We are excited to embark on this journey, and we hope that SVB will join us along the way.

Spokes Curriculum Outline
 
Format
Spokes will be teaching to students in a formal classroom setting, as well as to summer programs in libraries, community centers and camps. To best serve these different environments, we will offer half-day to full-day workshops to schools, and one- to two-day weekend workshops to summer programs outside of schools. The descriptions below list the classes that Spokes team members will be co-teaching with each other; we will combine the classes as appropriate for each environment we are in.
         Build and Drive a Raspberry Pi Car Workshop
        	In morning-afternoon or other two session style workshops, students will be exposed to basic ideas from mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, and computer science. In the first session, students will work with Raspberry Pi hardware and appropriate components to build a basic vehicle. This first workshop will introduce design thinking ideas, teaching students to think about why their devices are built the way they are and will familiarize them with working with components of mechanical and electrical devices.
        	In the afternoon, as a motivating exercise for basic exploration of the power of computer science, students will use the Pi programming language to interact with the hardware they constructed in the first session. The purpose of this session will be two-fold. First, students will be exposed to how humans interact with electronics and computer at a lower level than they usually do. For example, they will peel back the hood of a mechanical and electrical device and see how engineers designed the electronics to perform a specific function. The idea is that students learn that computers were not just “made”; real people designed and created the infrastructure and the software that lets them surf the web and play games, and creating these things is something they can learn how to do! Second, students will be exposed to the power of computers and a properly programmed device to interact with the physical world. To do this, students will program their vehicles to drive along a set course that they map out on the ground with tape. At the end we will host a competition to see which student or students made their car drive the course fastest or most efficiently!

Math and Games Workshop
During the mathematics workshops, students will be exposed to different concepts of game theory and asked to analyze the games that they played. Different topics will be introduced as games and students will have the opportunity to play against each other or even challenge the lecturer if time allows. Through hands-on exploration students will be able to devise winning strategies for games such as nim, chomp, and tic-tac-toe.
Furthermore, students will also be exposed to games with inherent positive or negative values associated with multiple different outcomes. Such games include the classic prisoner’s dilemma. Following exposure to these more complicated logic games, the class will conduct a simulation with candy as prizes. Additionally, a simulation will be demonstrated on Netlogo to depict the different possible outcomes on a larger sale.
This workshop will serve to encourage students to think critically and logically about mathematical concepts without experiencing the negative connotations that the word “math” often carries for them in school.

Circuits Workshop
Students will design and make electronic games using basic circuit elements, logic components, and a microcontroller. Students will be exposed to basic electrical engineering topics such as truth tables, Boolean algebra and circuit analysis. Students will also learn simple programming, circuit debugging and how to use a breadboard.
Possible games to design include rock paper scissors, whack-a-mole or another open-ended design. By leaving circuit kits at the schools, students can use what they learn to create more new games after the workshops end.

Climbing Mechanics
        	Through rock climbing students will gain intuition about mechanics principles such as force, torque and friction. By changing their body position and points of contact on a wall, students will directly experience forces and torques, and then do a free body analysis. Students can also learn about friction coefficients by experimenting with chalk. Biomechanics and hand anatomy will also be studied, exposing students to other mechanics topics including stress, and spring laws.
         Estimation Physics
        	How much energy do you output when you bicycle for an hour? How much power does a human output through heat? When watching a crazy stunt in a movie, do you wonder is that really possible? How could you check it?
        	Students will work with basic force balance and dimensional analysis to learn how to estimate things relevant to their daily lives. Emphasis will be placed on the process – asking the relevant questions, breaking the problem down into parts, making comparisons and order of magnitude estimates. These are good activities to brainstorm as a class, to have students compare separate answers, or to allow each student to explore their own interests.
 
Informal Activities (Outside the Workshops)
In addition to these official activities for students, we look to engage the broader communities we stay with through informal activities outside of the classroom. To help develop a community of tinkerers, we plan to use public spaces to demo creative and sustainable engineering projects, such as a bicycle-powered smoothie blender and other contraptions.  To encourage communities to be curious about the world around them, and the beauty contained therein, we will use a portable telescope for daytime observations of the sun and nighttime observations of the stars - topics which capture the minds of young and old alike. These projects would be easy to run, would reach more than just students, while generating interest in tinkering and exploring, showing that anyone can engage in their own explorations.

## Twitter: women in STEM follow-up (to Colin Crowell)

Colin Crowell
Twitter, Inc.

Dear Colin,
	I hope this document can address your concerns about Spokes’ plan for STEM outreach targeting girls this summer. I believe that Spokes and Twitter share a common goal, and I hope that this document can move our conversation about a partnership further by outlining our plans to engage with and excite young women about science, technology, engineering, and math. 
	To begin, Spokes is an outreach program designed to address the impending shortage of STEM professionals of all genders. We will be bringing exciting, hands-on science into classrooms and showing students how liberating science and tech can be. Students in Spokes workshops will have a different first experience with STEM than other students: our workshops will be fun, stress-free, engaging, and exciting, and, importantly, will take place absent of any pretenses that can discourage women from engaging in STEM later on. We’ll be helping all of our students succeed. Students won’t be graded. Their memories will be of making something amazing—not of feeling excluded or unsuccessful. Female students will have the opportunity to enjoy STEM outside of the structures that currently fail to attract them. 
	Second, at the beginning of each workshop we conduct, we intend to include a presentation explaining the larger significance of the topic and highlighting the work of leading scientists and engineers who are leaders in their field. In each of these presentations, we intend to include scientists from a balanced and diverse array of backgrounds so that all our students, including our female students, can see both that someone like them has succeeded in STEM and that STEM can take them anywhere no matter who they are. 
	Finally, Spokes will provide its female students with what is sometimes lacking in the foundation of their STEM education: role models and confidence. Out of our group of seven students, four are women, one of whom is our team leader. All are successful STEM students and will be continuing on to careers in STEM. All are excited about spreading the word about why they succeeded and why they love their fields. Female students will see these stories and know that they too can succeed with science and engineering, leaving them confident to let their interests grow. We’ll be showing them that science and engineering is cool and exciting and that it can be cool and exciting for anyone, including young women. 
	In addition to these tangible plans that are already a part of the Spokes plan, I recently made contact with Girls Who Code, an organization with which Twitter is already a partner, about established an outreach relationship, focused on spreading the word about Girls Who Code to schools in areas the program might not reach. 
	I hope this document has addressed your questions—preparing it was also helpful to us in making our goals for the summer more clear. I look forward to speaking to you in more depth about Spokes soon. 
 
Sincerely, 
Sam

## One-pager

Spokes America is a group of 7 MIT, Harvard, and Columbia University science and engineering students, with a mission to encourage school children across the US to be self-driven, creative and curious in their approach to science and engineering. Our team of 4 women and 3 men have experience in fields from engineering, mathematics, and computer science, in addition to teaching experience working with middle school students. Our journey will take us on a cross country cycling trip from San Francisco to Washington D.C. that will exemplify qualities of self-driven creativity and curiosity. 

The American education system is struggling to produce enough scientists, engineers, and technologists who can continue to drive technological development in the U.S. Spokes will address this critical issue by connecting students to the science that surrounds them, through hands-on, project-based workshops. Our workshops will encourage students to question and examine the world they live in and to use creative problem-solving to satisfy their own curiosity and to improve the world. 
STEM fields also suffer from negative stereotypes that push students away from these exciting fields—something akin to a “sexiness” vacuum. To fight these stereotypes and to show students that STEM is cool and that scientists can do anything and be anyone, we will be cycling between every stop on our journey. We want to show our students that they already have the tools to make real change in their lives using STEM, and to put them on a path to making waves across the entire world. 

The bottom line: Spokes America 2014 is enabling young people across the country to better realize their own creative problem-solving potential, as well as connecting teachers to new teaching resources for science and technology. Providing the US with the homegrown STEM professionals it needs starts here and now.

To learn more about Spokes America, please visit our website www.spokesamerica.org/. I look forward to discussing Spokes with you. Please also reach out to me at any time.
 
We are excited to embark on this adventure, and we hope that you’ll join us along the way.
