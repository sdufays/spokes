# Spokes MIT News Article - answers to MIT News questions (2022)

​​Questions for Spokes Team
 
Are all the current riders listed on this page?
Yes, except Keaten Clarno ended up not being able to join us of the biking portion on the trip. However, they helped us in planning for the trip, and they filed our receipts while we were on the road!
What made you decide to join Spokes and ride across the country?
For me, I had known a few other students who did Spokes in past years. In the paraphrased words of a friend who did Spokes in 2018, “I wouldn’t be who I am if I hadn’t done Spokes.” I also was very excited about each of the components of this trip. I love biking, although before this trip the most biking I had done was about 30 miles in a day. Additionally, I really enjoy working with kids and designing interesting workshops for students. And I love meeting people as I travel places. This trip is all of these things and more.
Where did you teach STEM Classes? Is the curriculum on this page up-to-date?
We taught Learning Festivals in a variety of locations including centers for adjudicated youth, summer schools, libraries, summer camps, and afterschool programs. The curriculum is slightly out of date on our website so here are more accurate descriptions of the workshops we taught:
Solar Cell: Have you ever wanted to make your own electricity? Using the power of plants? Well, now you can! In this workshop, students will build their own solar cells (aka a homemade solar panel), using raspberry and blackberry juice! We will learn about how the berry juice is able to interact with light to create electricity!
Color and Chromatography: What is color? How does it get there? We will explore some deceivingly complex questions in this workshop through simple experiments! Learn about the structural color that makes peacocks so bright, or the pigments present in leaves that are hidden from sight. All your questions about pigments, dyes, paints, and color are explored in this colorful workshop!
3D Printing: In this workshop, students learn about real-world applications of 3D printing, as well as the various 3D printing technologies that make them possible. Students will have hands-on exposure to cutting-edge, modern 3D printed parts, with parts printed on printers ranging in price from cheaper $180 printers to Sandford’s $1.8 Million medical 3D printer. Students will also help create a CAD-ed (computer automated drafting) part of their choosing! CAD-ed pieces have included Among Us characters, a Kirby, and a rocket!
Physics of Music: From workout playlists to love songs, music is an experience that shapes our emotions. It surrounds us in everyday life, and yet rarely are we shown the underlying decisions that make it so emotionally powerful. In this workshop, students learn about music through melody, harmony, timbre, and rhythm with a focus on the physics that underlies these fundamentals.
Circuitry: Electricity is everywhere! We use it to power everything, including our phones, laptops, television, and lights. But have you ever wondered how electricity works? Do you learn how to harness the power of electricity? Students will be able to build their own circuit to turn electricity into light! We will learn about how electricity works, different components of a circuit and the fundamentals of laws of electricity.
How were you received at the places you taught? What was the reaction to a group of MIT students riding and teaching?
We were received so graciously and generously at every place we stopped and taught (as well as the places we didn’t even teach, but just passed through)! The people we met along the way have been a major highlight of this trip, and this was especially true of the communities we taught in. The students were exuberant, curious, and excited. Our local contacts for the Learning Festivals often found us housing in the area, and they would give us suggestions of food or activities to check out while we were there. We had conversations with the students ranging from what type of shoes we would be if we were shoes to how they would apply to MIT if they wanted to. The “MIT” brand carries a lot of weight and prestige, especially in some of these small towns that we pass through. It has been awesome to connect with students and show them MIT is not an unreachable place, but a collection of curious and excited individuals like themselves.
How many places did you teach? How long were you there?
We taught in 9 locations (2 before the trip even started and 7 on the road):
Westborough, MA
Washington D.C.
Louisa County, VA
Hazard, KY
Bowling Green, KY
St. Louis, MO
Newton, KS
Lamar, CO
Fallon, NV
We taught in each location for just a day (except for 2 days in Hazard, KY). And we made it a tradition to get ice cream after every Learning Festival. We would usually ask the students we taught if they had any local ice cream recommendations (and they usually did)!
 
What did you do on your days off?
The joke was our rest days were never really rest days. We often had rest days in beautiful national parks (Bryce Canyon, Capitol Reef, Arches, etc.). And nearly every one of those days was spent doing something active: hiking, swimming, mountain biking, and even climbing through narrow metal structures at the City Museum in St. Louis. We did relax on a few rest days though. We spent a day in Farlington, KS, a tiny town on the Kansas-Missouri border. This day was an unintentional rest day, that came about when we discovered we had accidentally deleted a row (July 7th) from our master planning spreadsheet. We spent this day idling around Lake Crawford, fishing, eating curly fries in their one local diner, and keeping our heart rates below 100 bpm.
 
What was the hardest part of the ride?
On our third day of the trip, we encountered a MASSIVE climb up to Blue Ridge Parkway in VA. Our fresh, untoned leg muscles were not up for the challenge, and I ended up walking up most of this hill. The long, steep climbs are quite challenging (including the one up to Loveland Pass, where we crossed the Rockies), but strong headwinds are sometimes even more difficult than those climbs. Knowing you will crest the mountain is motivation for me on the uphills, but there’s no knowing when a headwind will let up! It’s psychological torture!
 
What was the best part of the ride?
The views! And the downhills! Whoever coined the phrase “things are going downhill” has clearly never been on a hilly bike ride. The downhills are so much fun. We get into this position called the “aero tuck” which is when you get very small on your bike to minimize drag. This allows us to fly down the hills! The views have also been absolutely stunning. We biked into the city of Black Hawk, CO early one morning. The backdrop of coniferous forests, with soft morning light, and a steep downhill was maybe the most beautiful view I’ve seen in my life!
What was the hottest day? What was the coldest day?
Hottest day: 105 in Lakin, KS. We stopped biking for the day when it got to be dangerously hot and ended up shuttling bikers in our support vehicle.
Coldest day: As we were biking down the descent of Loveland Pass (elev. 11,900 ft), it started raining and then hailing! The wind was whipping in our faces, and my fingers felt like they often feel on winter days in Boston when I forget to bring gloves on my rides.
10.  What was the longest ride?
We rode 113 miles from Austin, NV to Fallon, NV. This was part of the section of our ride along US Highway 50, branded the “loneliest road in America.” There is simply nothing between the tiny towns on this road. We were supposed to stay the night in one of those tiny towns between Austin and Fallon, called Middlegate. But we decided to bike straight to Fallon since the only (and I mean only) establishment in Middlegate is a hamburger joint/hostel. Fallon, a town of 8,500, felt like a bustling metropolis compared to the towns we had been staying in.
11. What muscles hurt the most at the end of the day?
After the first day, all of them. Over the next few weeks, we slowly built up the muscles required for endurance biking. Sore thighs were a common occurrence, as well as the less glamorous chafed butts.
12.  How many flat tires occurred on the ride?
About 10 or so between all six of our bikes over all 80 days! This is something we were quite proud of! Our bikes all had what are called tubeless tires, which essentially means there is no inner tube in the tire holding pressure, but the tire itself holds the pressure. This made repairing flats infinitely easier. Instead of having to take the tire off the rim and patch the inner tube when we got a flat, all we had to do was jam a little piece of rubber into the hole in our tire (called a tubeless plug), and we were good to go!
13.  How did it feel to finish the ride?
So special. Our journey finished as we crossed the Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco. I queued up some Vampire Weekend songs that remind me of early high school days and make me feel very sentimental, and I sobbed as we rode across the bridge. It was so cathartic. It is such a clear experience of putting in so much physical work to accomplish a huge goal. I felt so proud of myself and my teammates as we cycled across the bridge and into the arms of friends/family who greeted us at the finish line! I think I’ll always remember those final moments of the trip; they were very special.
14.  Would you do it again?
Absolutely. Maybe across a different country next (some of us have our eyes set on a Europe biking trip)! We also have already planned our next ride all together (biking to Provincetown, MA this October)!
15.  Are there any sponsors/people you would like to mention/thank?
So many of them. We couldn’t have done this trip without our sponsors and the very generous donors on our website. Very special thanks to two sponsors and supporters within the MIT community: the Edgerton Center and ESG (the Experimental Study Group). MIT Spokes is part of the Edgerton Center (along with other amazing clubs like Rocket Team, Solar Car, and more). This trip would not have been possible without the help and support from Sandi Lipnoski in the Edgerton Center. Sandi, thank you for answering our texts about rental car crises at 10 pm on weekends. We appreciate everything you have done for our team!
16.  Is there anything that I didn’t ask you that you would like to mention?
Yes! A few things:
Warm Showers: Warm Showers is a website that is very similar to Couch Surfing, but it is meant for touring cyclists. People all over the country open up their homes to bicyclists on the road, and we stayed with many of these people along our trip. Everyone we met through Warm Showers was kind, supportive, helpful, and so generous. Thank you very much to all of our wonderful Warm Showers hosts!
Support vehicle: We do this trip with a support vehicle (our lovely Ford Explorer rental car), and we switch off driving the car each day. We need the car to transport our luggage, our Learning Festival materials, and other items!
Mayor Jerry: We had an especially delightful time staying in the tiny town of 400 people in Wayland, KY. We stayed with the mayor of this town, Jerry Fultz and his wife King. Jerry and King are amazing; they care so deeply about the town. The phone number on Mayor Jerry’s business cards is his personal cell number. He told us that residents not only call his cell phone if they have a problem, but they regularly show up on his front porch. And he welcomes this because he cares so much about each resident of Wayland. Wayland is also the home of a local legendary basketball player, “King” Kelly Coleman. Wayland is a time capsule to Kelly Coleman, as well as the coal mining history of the town.
